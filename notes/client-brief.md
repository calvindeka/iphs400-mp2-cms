# Client brief

**Client:** Knox County Historical Society (course default, with one added
detail — see note at bottom).

**Who runs the site, and how comfortable are they with computers?**
Three volunteers. Karen, the secretary, posts meeting announcements and event
notices weekly; she's comfortable with email and not much else — no patience
for anything that looks like "software." Tom, the president, writes longer
research pieces every month or two and wants them to read as permanent,
citable work, not throwaway posts. The third volunteer handles dues and
member records; she runs a short weekly check-in with Karen and Tom to decide
what needs posting that week, attends every society meeting and event in
person, and has shadowed both of them long enough to cover either role if one
is unavailable.

**Who reads it, and what are they usually trying to find?**
Members and prospective members. The three things people ask about every
month, without fail: when is the next meeting, where is it, and what's on the
agenda. A smaller group reads Tom's research pieces and wants to find them
again months later — those need permanent-feeling URLs, not something buried
in a feed.

**What is broken about how they do it now?**
Their current site is a Facebook page. It isn't searchable, so nobody can
find Tom's older pieces or last quarter's meeting notes — everything is
public and permanent the moment it posts, with no way to organize it.

**What would make them say "this is better"?**
Karen can post a meeting announcement in under two minutes without help.
Tom's research pieces keep their formatting and get a permanent, memorable
URL he can hand out. The three recurring questions (when/where/agenda) are
answerable from the homepage without scrolling a feed.

**What must not happen?**
- Nobody but Tom edits or deletes the membership/leadership content.
- A draft (an unfinished research piece, a not-yet-final agenda) must never
  be visible to the public, even briefly.
